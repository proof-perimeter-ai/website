RBI and DPDP compliance for document AI used to mean two narrow checks: a data-residency clause and an outsourcing due-diligence form. That's no longer accurate. Since the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 were notified on November 13, 2025, every document AI vendor handling a KYC packet, loan file, or claims bundle for an Indian bank, NBFC, or insurer sits inside a data-protection statute with real teeth, layered on top of the Reserve Bank of India's existing Master Direction on Outsourcing of IT Services — and, as of August 2025, a third framework: RBI's own FREE-AI report setting governance expectations for the AI models themselves. For a procurement team, that's three questions converging on one vendor contract: where is the data processed, who governs the model reading it, and can both survive an examiner's request for evidence. This guide walks through what each framework actually requires.

## What Is India's Regulatory Perimeter for Document AI, and Why Does It Cover Vendors?

Three distinct Indian regimes now bear on a document AI deployment, each asking a different question. The [Reserve Bank of India's Master Direction on Outsourcing of Information Technology Services](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_ViewMasDirections.aspx?id=12486), effective October 1, 2023, treats any IT service a regulated entity outsources — including a document AI platform that classifies or extracts data from a regulated document — as an outsourcing arrangement subject to risk-management, contractual, and audit requirements. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, fully operationalized once its 2025 Rules were notified, regulates the personal data inside those documents directly, independent of any outsourcing relationship. And RBI's Framework for Responsible and Ethical Enablement of Artificial Intelligence (FREE-AI), released August 13, 2025, sets a third layer specifically for the AI model itself — governance neither of the other two frameworks was written to cover. A vendor that satisfies only one of the three has answered a third of the question a compliance team will actually ask.

## What Does RBI's Outsourcing Master Direction Actually Require in a Contract?

The Master Direction applies to commercial banks, co-operative banks, NBFCs, credit information companies, and All India Financial Institutions, and it treats a document AI platform no differently from any other outsourced IT function the moment it touches a KYC or loan file on the institution's behalf.

### Cross-Border Processing Requires Jurisdictional Risk Monitoring, Not Just a Storage Promise

Where a service provider — or a sub-processor behind it — sits in a different jurisdiction, the regulated entity must actively monitor that jurisdiction's political, legal, and economic conditions on an ongoing basis, not simply confirm a storage region at signing. A vendor that can name where data sits but can't speak to the operating jurisdiction of the model reading the document hasn't closed this requirement.

### Audit and Inspection Rights Must Reach the Service Provider — and RBI Itself

The regulated entity and RBI must each retain the right to audit or inspect the service provider, including in a foreign jurisdiction. A platform built on a resold, general-purpose frontier model typically can't extend that inspection right into infrastructure it doesn't operate — it's reselling an API relationship, not the auditable environment the Master Direction expects.

### Exit Strategy and Data-Availability Guarantees Have to Survive the Vendor's Own Failure

The Direction requires a documented contingency and exit strategy, including a guarantee that institution records remain available even if the service provider is liquidated. For a document AI vendor, the extraction history and provenance trail behind every processed document — not just the raw source files — has to be portable, not locked inside a platform that could disappear.

## What Does the DPDP Act's 2025 Rules Add Beyond Outsourcing?

The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 answer a question RBI's outsourcing framework doesn't: what's actually allowed to happen to the personal data itself, regardless of the contractual relationship around it.

### Cross-Border Transfer Runs on a Negative List, Not an Adequacy Whitelist

[Rule 15 of the DPDP Rules, 2025](https://www.meity.gov.in/documents/act-and-policies/digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025-gDOxUjMtQWa?pageTitle=Digital-Personal-Data-Protection-Rules-2025686cadad39.pdf) takes a structurally different approach from GDPR's adequacy-decision model or DORA's processing-location disclosure requirement: personal data can be transferred outside India by default, restricted only where the central government specifically names a country or condition. That's a lower bar to clear on paper, but it cuts the other way for a document AI vendor's due-diligence story — "we're not on a restricted list" is a weaker representation than "we can name the jurisdiction our processing runs in and why," and an institution's risk committee is likely to ask for the latter regardless of what the rule technically requires.

### Significant Data Fiduciary Status Raises the Bar Sharply

Where a document AI vendor's processing volume or sensitivity meets the threshold for designation as a Significant Data Fiduciary, the Act adds mandatory data protection impact assessments and periodic data audits — well beyond what a standard vendor contract typically covers. A bank's KYC or loan-processing volume plausibly pushes a vendor toward that threshold on its own.

## What Does RBI's FREE-AI Framework Add to AI Governance for Document Processing?

Where the outsourcing rules govern the arrangement and the DPDP Act governs the data, [RBI's FREE-AI report](https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=59377) is aimed squarely at the model. Built around seven guiding principles — including trust, fairness, accountability, and resilience — and organized into six pillars (Infrastructure, Policy, Capacity, Governance, Protection, and Assurance), its 26 recommendations call for board-approved AI policies, AI-specific consumer-protection mechanisms, and audit trails for AI-driven decisions. It's advisory rather than binding today, but RBI's public posture since makes clear it isn't optional in practice: speaking at the FIBAC 2026 banking conference in Mumbai on August 11, 2026, Governor Sanjay Malhotra told financial institutions that [DPDP compliance alone isn't sufficient governance for AI](https://www.business-standard.com/industry/news/rbi-governor-malhotra-only-ai-can-help-limit-ai-frauds-126081100730_1.html), and that banks need complete AI inventories, board-approved policies with clear accountability, the ability to explain a lending or fraud decision after the fact, and stress-testing before and after deployment. For a document AI system feeding a credit or KYC decision, that's a direct description of what a vendor has to produce — not a future milestone.

## How Do RBI's Outsourcing Rules, FREE-AI, and DPDP Interact for a Document AI Vendor?

The three frameworks ask different, overlapping questions rather than one flat checklist. Outsourcing asks whether the *arrangement* — the contract, the sub-processor chain, the exit plan — is properly governed. FREE-AI asks whether the *model* — its inventory status, explainability, stress-test history — is properly governed. DPDP asks whether the *personal data itself* is handled lawfully, independent of either. Full RBI and DPDP compliance for document AI means all three records exist together: an outsourcing contract with airtight audit-rights language means little if the institution can't also produce a Significant Data Fiduciary impact assessment or explain why a field was extracted a specific way.

Only partially — and that gap is worth surfacing before a vendor enters a procurement pipeline. A platform reselling a licensed frontier model can describe its own controls, but it can rarely extend audit access, sub-processor visibility, or stress-test documentation into infrastructure it doesn't operate — precisely the visibility RBI's outsourcing rules and FREE-AI both ask for. ## A Practical RBI/DPDP Readiness Checklist for Document AI Procurement

- **Get the processing jurisdiction in writing, not just the storage region** — and ask the vendor to name it specifically enough that your risk committee can monitor it on an ongoing basis, per the Master Direction's requirement.
- **Confirm the audit right is real.** Can the vendor grant inspection access to where inference actually happens, including to RBI directly, or only to the layer it controls?
- **Ask whether your volume pushes the vendor toward Significant Data Fiduciary status**, and if so, whether it can already produce a data protection impact assessment, not draft one after the fact.
- **Request the vendor's AI inventory entry and stress-test history** — the specific evidence Governor Malhotra named at FIBAC 2026 — not just a general accuracy claim.
- **Check whether [SOC 2 controls](/glossary/soc-2-document-controls) and [data-residency](/glossary/data-residency-in-document-ai) commitments cover inference, not just storage** — all three Indian frameworks reach further than either document typically does alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the DPDP Act apply to a document AI vendor that isn't itself based in India?

Yes. The Act applies to the processing of digital personal data of individuals in India, regardless of where the processing entity is located, if the processing is connected to offering goods or services within India — a document AI vendor processing an Indian bank's KYC packets falls squarely inside that scope.

### Is RBI's FREE-AI framework legally binding today?

Not yet — it's a report of recommendations, not a binding master direction, so there's no direct penalty for non-compliance today. But RBI's regulated entities are already expected to treat its direction as the baseline, and Governor Malhotra's August 2026 remarks make clear supervisory expectations are moving faster than formal rulemaking on this point.

### Does a regional data-storage guarantee satisfy RBI and DPDP requirements for a document AI vendor?

No. RBI's outsourcing rules separately require jurisdictional risk monitoring and real audit rights, the DPDP Act adds lawful cross-border transfer and, potentially, Significant Data Fiduciary obligations, and FREE-AI adds model-level governance and explainability expectations. A storage commitment alone answers none of the three fully.

## The Takeaway

RBI and DPDP compliance for document AI isn't a single certification a vendor can produce — it's three records converging on one accountability question: who governs the arrangement, who governs the model, and who governs the data, and can the institution show all three to an examiner on request. A platform that runs inference inside the institution's own perimeter answers that question directly, rather than trying to document access to infrastructure — and a sub-processor chain — it doesn't fully control.
