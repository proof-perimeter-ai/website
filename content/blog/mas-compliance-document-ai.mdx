Since December 11, 2024, MAS compliance for document AI has meant something more specific than "keep customer data in Singapore." That's the date the Monetary Authority of Singapore's revised Guidelines on Outsourcing took effect, replacing the 2016 version banks and financial institutions had built their vendor-review programs around for nearly a decade. A year later, MAS went further: a November 2025 consultation paper proposing dedicated Guidelines on AI Risk Management, which reaches specifically into how a document AI system is built, documented, and monitored — not just where the vendor operating it sits. Together, the two frameworks describe what a Singapore bank, insurer, or lender actually has to prove about a document AI vendor, and it's considerably more than a data-residency attestation.

## What Is MAS, and Why Does It Regulate Document AI Vendors?

The Monetary Authority of Singapore is both the country's central bank and its integrated financial regulator — it supervises banks, insurers, capital markets intermediaries, and payment institutions under one roof, which is part of why its guidelines move quickly and reach broadly. A document AI platform that classifies a KYC packet, extracts figures from a loan file, or reads a claims bundle for a Singapore-regulated entity is, functionally, outsourced ICT infrastructure the moment it touches that entity's regulated data. MAS's Guidelines on Outsourcing apply from there — and its emerging AI-specific guidance layers on top for any system that qualifies as AI, which is nearly every modern document extraction platform. That's what makes MAS compliance for document AI a two-part question, not a single vendor checklist.

## What Does MAS Compliance for Document AI Actually Require in a Contract?

The [Guidelines on Outsourcing (Financial Institutions other than Banks)](https://www.mas.gov.sg/regulation/guidelines/guidelines-on-outsourcing-financial-institutions-other-than-banks) and their bank-specific equivalent, alongside Notices 658 and 1121, took effect on December 11, 2024, replacing the prior 2016 guidelines. They set out what MAS expects an institution to have in place before — and throughout — an outsourcing relationship, document AI included.

### The Materiality Assessment That Decides How Much Scrutiny Applies

MAS doesn't treat every vendor relationship the same. Institutions run a materiality assessment on each outsourcing arrangement, and MAS is explicit that this is a matter of qualitative judgment, not a fixed checklist: an arrangement that starts out non-material can become material later, purely from an increase in document volume or an expansion in what the vendor handles. A document AI deployment that begins as a pilot on one document type and quietly grows into full KYC-file processing across every branch is exactly the drift MAS expects an institution to catch and reclassify — not something that stays "low-risk" because that's how the contract was originally scoped.

### The Outsourcing Register — and Who Has to See It

Institutions carrying out outsourced relevant services are expected to maintain an inventory of those arrangements and submit it to MAS on a recurring basis, or on request. A document AI vendor a bank hasn't logged in that inventory is a gap the moment MAS asks — independent of how well the vendor is actually performing. Underneath the register requirement sits an audit expectation: MAS and the institution both need the ability to inspect the service provider directly, not just receive a compliance attestation from it secondhand. Neither the register nor the audit right is optional scaffolding — they're the baseline MAS compliance for document AI has to clear before AIRG's system-level questions even come into play.

## What Does MAS's New AI Risk Management Framework Add for Document AI Systems?

The Outsourcing Guidelines regulate the vendor relationship. MAS's [proposed Guidelines on AI Risk Management](https://www.mas.gov.sg/news/media-releases/2025/mas-guidelines-for-artificial-intelligence-risk-management) (AIRG), published for consultation on November 13, 2025 and open for comment until January 31, 2026, regulate the system itself — a materially different question for a document AI deployment, since the model's behavior, not just the vendor's operations, is what's now in scope.

### Life-Cycle Documentation: Model Cards, Data Lineage, and Test Reports

The proposed guidelines are specific about what an institution should be able to produce: model cards, [data lineage](/glossary/data-lineage-in-document-processing) records, and test reports covering how a model's training and evaluation data was sourced and assessed as fit-for-purpose, plus version control and rollback mechanisms as the model changes. For a document AI system, that's a deeper ask than a vendor's usual accuracy benchmark slide — a record of *why* a field extraction can be trusted, not just a claim that it usually is.

### Materiality Tiers and Explainability Thresholds

Like the outsourcing framework, AIRG is proportionate: institutions are expected to maintain an AI inventory, run a materiality assessment per use case, and apply controls scaled to that risk across five life-cycle areas — data management, fairness, transparency and explainability, human oversight, and third-party AI management. A document AI system deciding whether a KYC packet passes verification, or feeding figures into an underwriting decision, sits toward the high-materiality end of that scale — meaning a stronger explainability threshold and more rigorous independent validation than a low-stakes internal tool would need.

### The 12-Month Transition Window Isn't a Reason to Wait

MAS has proposed a 12-month transition period once the guidelines are finalized — a real runway, but one that assumes an institution starts building the documentation trail now rather than after the consultation closes. A document AI vendor that can't already produce data lineage records and per-field provenance isn't twelve months away from being ready; it's starting the clock later than a procurement team may realize.

## Does MAS's Oversight Reach a Document AI Vendor's Subcontractors?

In principle, yes — the outsourcing framework's audit expectations and AIRG's third-party AI management control area both assume an institution can see into the arrangement it actually relies on, not just the contract's first layer. In practice, this is where a platform built on a resold frontier model runs into trouble: it can describe its own operations, but typically can't produce the model card, training-data lineage, or independent-validation record for a third-party model it licenses rather than operates. A one-line "powered by a leading AI model" disclosure satisfies neither MAS's materiality-driven scrutiny nor the [audit-ready documentation](/glossary/audit-ready-document-workflows) AIRG is proposing to require.

## How Does MAS Compare to DORA, RBI, and SAMA on Document AI Oversight?

MAS's two-framework approach — outsourcing rules for the vendor relationship, AIRG for the system's behavior — lands roughly where the EU's DORA and AI Act land together, covered in more depth in [our dedicated guide to DORA compliance for document AI](/blog/dora-compliance-document-ai). Where MAS differs from India's RBI and Saudi Arabia's SAMA, both leaning on in-country hosting defaults, is that AIRG is less about *where* a model runs and more about whether an institution can document what it does and why — a gap that matters even for a vendor that already satisfies data residency but has never produced a model card. That tracks how analysts already rate this market: Gartner's [inaugural Magic Quadrant for Intelligent Document Processing Solutions](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/6912666) (September 2025) places the major cloud hyperscalers as Challengers, not Leaders, largely on governance criteria — the same gap AIRG targets. ## A Practical MAS Readiness Checklist for Document AI Procurement

- **Treat MAS compliance for document AI as two reviews, not one** — the outsourcing assessment covers the vendor relationship; AIRG's controls cover what the model itself can be shown to do.
- **Run the materiality assessment as an ongoing exercise**, not a one-time gate — volume and scope creep can turn a "low-risk" pilot into a material arrangement unnoticed.
- **Confirm the vendor is in your outsourcing inventory**, with the documentation MAS expects to see on request.
- **Ask for a model card and data lineage record now**, not after AIRG's consultation closes — the transition period rewards institutions that already have this evidence.
- **Check whether [SOC 2 controls](/glossary/soc-2-document-controls) cover the model's behavior or only the vendor's infrastructure** — those are different questions under AIRG.
- **Trace the subcontracting chain.** If the platform licenses a third-party frontier model, ask who can actually produce that model's documentation.

A [demo call](/book-demo) is a faster way to test these answers than a compliance FAQ — bring a KYC packet or loan file and ask, specifically, who can produce a model card for the system reading it. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does MAS's outsourcing regime apply to a document AI vendor that isn't itself a licensed financial institution?

Yes. MAS regulates the financial institution directly, but its Guidelines on Outsourcing require that institution to run due diligence, materiality assessment, and register the arrangement for any outsourced service provider it relies on — document AI vendors included, regardless of the vendor's own regulatory status.

### Is MAS's AI Risk Management guideline legally binding yet?

Not yet. As of this writing, the Guidelines on AI Risk Management are a consultation paper open for comment until January 31, 2026, with a proposed 12-month transition period once finalized. They aren't enforceable today, but institutions that wait until finalization to start building the required documentation will be starting the transition clock later than they need to.

### Does storing documents within Singapore satisfy MAS's requirements for a document AI deployment?

Not by itself. Data residency addresses the Outsourcing Guidelines' location questions, but it says nothing about the model documentation, explainability, and human-oversight controls the proposed AI Risk Management guidelines expect for the system's actual behavior — those are a separate set of obligations layered on top.

## The Takeaway

MAS compliance for document AI now runs on two tracks: the Outsourcing Guidelines govern the vendor relationship, and the proposed AI Risk Management guidelines govern the system itself — model cards, data lineage, explainability, and human oversight, scaled to materiality. A document AI vendor that can already produce that documentation, because inference runs inside the institution's own perimeter rather than through a subcontracted model it can't fully explain, is the difference between reacting to AIRG once it's final and having already met it.
