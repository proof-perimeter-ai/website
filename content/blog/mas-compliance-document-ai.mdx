Singapore's document AI vendors have spent the past two years building compliance answers around a regime that's already partway out the door. MAS compliance for document AI meant, until recently, satisfying the Guidelines on Outsourcing that took effect December 11, 2024 — due diligence, a register of arrangements, audit rights, the usual outsourcing checklist. Then, on March 6, 2026, the Monetary Authority of Singapore published a consultation paper proposing to supersede those very guidelines with a broader Third-Party Risk Management framework, while a separate November 2025 consultation on AI-specific risk management runs in parallel. A bank that only re-reads its outsourcing contract once a year is compliant with a regime that's being rewritten underneath it. This guide walks through what MAS actually requires of a document AI vendor today, what's about to change, and what a procurement team needs in writing either way.

## What Is MAS Compliance for Document AI, and Why Does It Apply to Vendors?

A document AI platform — anything that classifies, extracts, or reads a KYC packet, a loan file, or a claims bundle for a bank, insurer, or other financial institution regulated by MAS — is an outsourcing arrangement (and, increasingly, a third-party AI risk) the moment it processes a regulated document on the institution's behalf. There's no size exemption for "just a document tool," and no carve-out for AI specifically until the newer AI-focused guidance is finalized. The institution, not the vendor, carries the compliance obligation to MAS — a document AI contract that doesn't anticipate that is a gap the institution inherits, not the vendor.

## What Do MAS's Outsourcing Guidelines Require From a Document AI Vendor Today?

The [Guidelines on Outsourcing (Banks)](https://www.mas.gov.sg/regulation/guidelines/guidelines-on-outsourcing-banks) and their equivalent for non-bank financial institutions, both effective December 11, 2024, are still the operative regime for MAS compliance for document AI procurement as of this writing, alongside Notice 658 (banks) and Notice 1121 (merchant banks) on managing outsourced relevant services.

### When Does a Document AI Vendor Count as a "Material" Outsourcing Arrangement?

MAS classifies an arrangement as a **Material Ongoing Outsourced Relevant Service (MOORS)** when a service provider's failure could materially affect the institution's business, customers, financial soundness, or ability to comply with regulatory requirements. Most KYC, loan-file, or claims-extraction document AI deployments clear that bar directly — these are workflows a compliance decision or a customer onboarding step actually depends on, not a peripheral back-office tool. Once a vendor is classified as material, due diligence has to happen before the arrangement starts and again within 24 months of go-live, or at whatever cadence the institution's board approves — a document AI vendor that can't support a repeatable due-diligence review isn't built for this classification, whatever its extraction accuracy looks like in a demo.

### What the Outsourcing Register and Audit Rights Actually Require

Institutions must maintain a register of all outsourcing arrangements, including material ones, and submit it to MAS on a regular basis. A document AI vendor that hasn't been logged in that register the moment it starts touching regulated documents is a live compliance gap, independent of how well the extraction performs. Contracts entered or renewed after December 11, 2023 also have to include the institution's — and MAS's — right to audit the service provider, plus a defined right to terminate under specified circumstances. A vendor that can only offer audit access to the layer it directly controls, and not to whatever infrastructure actually processes the document, doesn't satisfy this in practice, even if the contract language technically checks the box.

## How Will MAS's New Third-Party Risk Management Guidelines Change This?

This is the part most document AI compliance content published elsewhere doesn't cite, because it's recent: on March 6, 2026, MAS opened a consultation on proposed [Guidelines on Third-Party Risk Management](https://www.rajahtannasia.com/viewpoints/mas-proposes-new-third-party-risk-management-guidelines-to-cover-all-third-party-services/), which would supersede the current Outsourcing Guidelines entirely and widen their scope from outsourced services specifically to **all** third-party arrangements a financial institution relies on. The consultation closed April 20, 2026, and final guidelines — with an expected six-month transition period — are pending as this post is published. Bank-specific Notices 658 and 1121 stay in force regardless of how the broader TPRM Guidelines land.

For document AI, that scope widening matters more than it might first appear: a platform reselling a third-party frontier model's inference is a third-party arrangement whether or not the institution's procurement team ever labeled it "outsourcing." A document AI vendor's contract written narrowly to satisfy the current Outsourcing Guidelines may not automatically satisfy a TPRM framework built to reach every third-party dependency, including the subcontracting and concentration-risk questions the current regime addresses less directly — which makes MAS compliance for document AI a moving target for any contract signed before the transition period closes.

## What Does MAS's AI Risk Management Consultation Add on Top of Outsourcing?

Running alongside the TPRM proposal is a separate track: MAS's [consultation paper on Guidelines on AI Risk Management](https://www.mas.gov.sg/news/media-releases/2025/mas-guidelines-for-artificial-intelligence-risk-management), published November 13, 2025 and closing January 31, 2026, which sets supervisory expectations specifically for how financial institutions govern, test, monitor, and manage AI risk — generative AI and autonomous AI agents included. The proposed expectations span data management, transparency and explainability, human oversight, third-party AI risk management, model evaluation and testing, and post-deployment monitoring, applied proportionately to the institution's size and AI use.

For a document AI deployment, the data-management and explainability expectations land directly on [provenance](/glossary/data-lineage-in-document-processing): a vendor has to be able to show what a model saw and decided, field by field, not just claim an aggregate accuracy number. ## How Does MAS Interact With Singapore's PDPA for Document AI?

Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act runs on a separate track from MAS's sector-specific rules, but the two overlap directly for document AI: most KYC packets, loan files, and claims documents contain personal data the PDPA governs regardless of the financial-sector overlay. The PDPA permits cross-border data transfer where the receiving jurisdiction offers comparable protection and the institution can demonstrate contractual and technical safeguards — a standard that a document AI vendor sending documents to an offshore inference endpoint has to satisfy on top of, not instead of, whatever MAS's outsourcing or AI risk guidelines separately require. A vendor that treats "PDPA-compliant" as equivalent to "MAS-compliant" is answering a narrower question than the one an examiner will actually ask.

## A Practical Compliance Checklist for Document AI Procurement in Singapore

A short checklist covers most of what MAS compliance for document AI actually comes down to in a vendor contract today:

- **Confirm materiality classification first.** Most KYC, underwriting, and claims extraction workflows qualify as MOORS — get the due-diligence and audit-rights obligations that follow from that classification in writing, not assumed.
- **Ask how the vendor is tracking the TPRM Guidelines transition**, not just the current Outsourcing Guidelines — a contract built only for today's regime may need renegotiation once the final TPRM standard and its transition period land.
- **Request a real audit right**, reaching the environment where inference actually happens, not just the layer the vendor directly operates.
- **Ask for [provenance](/glossary/document-audit-trail), not just a [SOC 2 report](/glossary/soc-2-document-controls).** MAS's AI risk management expectations on data governance and explainability go further than a general security-controls attestation.
- **Check the PDPA cross-border transfer basis separately** from MAS outsourcing sign-off — the two obligations don't collapse into one compliance step.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does MAS's outsourcing regime still apply if the new Third-Party Risk Management Guidelines haven't been finalized yet?

Yes. The current Guidelines on Outsourcing, effective December 11, 2024, remain the operative regime until MAS finalizes and phases in the proposed TPRM Guidelines, expected with a six-month transition period after issuance. Institutions can't treat the pending consultation as a reason to defer current obligations.

### Is a document AI vendor automatically a "material" outsourcing arrangement under MAS's rules?

Not automatically, but most KYC, loan-file, and claims-extraction deployments meet the Material Ongoing Outsourced Relevant Service bar, since a failure would materially affect the institution's business, customers, or regulatory compliance. Institutions should assess materiality per arrangement rather than assuming a document AI tool is exempt by default.

### Does complying with Singapore's PDPA satisfy MAS's document AI compliance expectations?

No. The PDPA governs cross-border transfer of personal data specifically; MAS's outsourcing and AI risk management guidelines impose separate, sector-specific obligations around due diligence, audit rights, and data governance that a PDPA transfer basis alone doesn't cover.

## The Takeaway

MAS compliance for document AI isn't a single checklist anymore — it's a regime in transition: the current Outsourcing Guidelines govern today's contracts, a Third-Party Risk Management framework is set to widen and replace them, and a parallel AI risk management standard adds data-governance and explainability expectations on top of both. A document AI vendor that can show real audit access, field-level provenance, and a defined inference boundary answers all three tracks at once, instead of renegotiating a new contract clause every time MAS finalizes the next stage of this transition.
