Most regulators ask an institution to justify where its data goes. The Gulf's two largest financial regulators invert that: the data stays, and leaving is what needs justifying. SAMA and CBUAE compliance for document AI therefore starts from a different place than an EU or Singapore review — Saudi Arabia expects cloud workloads to sit in-Kingdom unless SAMA has approved otherwise, and the Central Bank of the UAE requires a bank's Master System of Record, all Confidential Data included, to be continuously maintained inside the UAE. For a bank or insurer evaluating a document AI vendor, that reshapes the procurement conversation, because a cloud extraction API is an offshore processing arrangement by construction. Here's what both regimes require, and what has to be in writing before signing.

## Why Do Gulf Regulators Reach Past Storage Into Processing?

Both regimes were written around a supervisory concern rather than a privacy one: a regulator has to be able to reach the data, the records, and the provider, on demand, in its own jurisdiction. That framing is why "we store your documents in a local region" answers less of the question than it appears to. A [KYC packet](/glossary/kyc-know-your-customer) staged in Riyadh or Dubai and then sent to a model-serving endpoint in another country has been processed abroad, whatever the storage bucket's label says — and processing abroad is exactly the event both frameworks gate.

## What Does SAMA Require Before a Saudi Bank Uses a Document AI Vendor?

### In-Kingdom by Default, Offshore Only With SAMA's Approval

SAMA does not impose an absolute ban on data leaving Saudi Arabia, but it does invert the default: cloud services are expected to run in-Kingdom, and explicit SAMA approval is required to use cloud services located outside it. In practice, a Saudi-region endpoint isn't automatically sufficient either — residency depends on where content actually resides and transits across its whole lifecycle, [including backup, replication, and disaster-recovery failover](https://www.kiteworks.com/regulatory-compliance/sama-data-sovereignty-hosting-compliance/). A vendor whose inference tier fails over to a foreign region under load has an in-Kingdom answer that only holds on a good day.

### Material Outsourcing Needs a Prior No-Objection

Materiality gates a second, separate obligation. Where an outsourcing arrangement is material — and core KYC, AML, or underwriting document extraction generally is — SAMA's prior no-objection is required before the institution proceeds. That means a document AI deployment can face two distinct approval paths: one because it is cloud, one because it is material. Vendors accustomed to a self-service signup flow are frequently unprepared for either.

### PDPL Article 29 Adds a Second, Non-Financial Gate

Saudi Arabia's Personal Data Protection Law regulates the same transfer from outside the financial rulebook. Article 29 permits transfer or disclosure of personal data outside the Kingdom only for defined purposes and only where the transfer doesn't prejudice national security or the Kingdom's vital interests. SDAIA's Regulation on Personal Data Transfer Outside the Kingdom, updated in September 2024, layers on required safeguards — standard contractual clauses, binding common rules, or an accreditation certificate — plus a transfer risk assessment, and [SDAIA's February 2025 risk assessment guidelines](https://www.clydeco.com/en/insights/2025/03/update-on-saudi-arabia-risk-assessment-guidelines) set out how that assessment is expected to be run. A bank that clears SAMA's cloud approval still owes a documented, data-minimised transfer assessment under the PDPL if documents are processed abroad.

## What Does CBUAE Require of a Document AI Arrangement in the UAE?

The UAE's rules are, if anything, more literal.

### The Master System of Record Has to Stay Onshore

Under the CBUAE Outsourcing Regulation for Banks, a bank must ensure its Master System of Record — which includes all Confidential Data — is continuously maintained and stored within the UAE. Branches of foreign banks get a narrow exception, subject to Central Bank approval, by keeping a UAE copy updated at least daily. There is no version of this requirement that a wholly offshore document pipeline satisfies by accident.

### Confidential Data Leaving the UAE Needs Two Approvals, Not One

[Article 6 of the regulation](https://rulebook.centralbank.ae/en/rulebook/article-6-outsourcing-outside-uae) is explicit that a bank's customers' Confidential Data must not be shared outside the UAE without Central Bank approval *and* the customer's prior written consent. The second condition is the one most cloud document AI architectures cannot practically meet: obtaining per-customer written consent to send their identity documents to a foreign inference endpoint is not a checkbox in an onboarding flow, it is a product decision with legal weight.

### Some Jurisdictions Are Off the Table Entirely

The same article bars outsourcing arrangements that propose storing data in any jurisdiction where bank secrecy or other laws restrict access to data needed for supervisory purposes, and requires that the bank and its customers retain ownership of the data with the Central Bank able to access it on request. It also asks banks to actively manage the risk that economic, political, legal, or regulatory change in the provider's jurisdiction could impair the arrangement — a country-risk assessment on the vendor's location, not just its security posture.

## What Does CBUAE's AI Guidance Note Add Beyond the Outsourcing Rules?

The CBUAE has also published a [guidance note on consumer protection and the responsible adoption and use of AI and machine learning](https://rulebook.centralbank.ae/en/rulebook/guidance-note-consumer-protection-and-responsible-adoption-and-use-artificial-intelligence) by licensed financial institutions. Its principles — governance and accountability, fairness and non-discrimination, transparency, continuous monitoring and review, effective human oversight, and data quality, privacy, and security — track closely to what MAS proposed for Singapore, and they expect a documented AI governance framework proportionate to the institution's size and complexity.

The principles are guidance rather than binding rules, which makes them easy to under-weight and expensive to ignore: they describe the evidence a supervisor will look for when an AI-assisted decision is questioned. For document AI specifically, "effective human oversight" and "transparency" are not abstractions — they translate into whether a low-confidence extraction routed to a reviewer, and whether the institution can show what the model saw when it produced a value that ended up in a credit or onboarding decision.

## Where Does SAMA and CBUAE Compliance for Document AI Break Down in Practice?

Almost always at the same place: a platform that resells a licensed frontier model can commit to a storage region but not to where inference physically executes, cannot name the sub-processor that served a given request, and cannot pass through the supervisory access both regulators require. [Gartner's inaugural Magic Quadrant for Intelligent Document Processing Solutions](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/6912666), published September 2025, is worth reading with that in mind — most vendors evaluated there were built cloud-first, with deployment flexibility retrofitted rather than architected in, which is why the Gulf's onshore-by-default posture tends to surface as an exception request rather than a straightforward yes.

On Proof Perimeter's internal benchmarks, that fine-tuned model delivers 20% higher accuracy and 50% lower token consumption than general-purpose frontier models on the same document-extraction tasks, and every extracted field carries [field-level provenance](/glossary/data-lineage-in-document-processing) — what the model saw, what it decided, and why. That record is the artifact a SAMA or CBUAE examiner can actually inspect, and the same one the CBUAE guidance note's transparency and human-oversight principles ask an institution to produce.

## A Practical Gulf Readiness Checklist for Document AI Procurement

- **Ask where inference runs across the full lifecycle** — steady state, failover, backup, and disaster recovery — not just where the primary region is set.
- **Establish which approvals apply before you negotiate.** In Saudi Arabia, a cloud arrangement and a material outsourcing arrangement can each require their own SAMA sign-off.
- **For UAE deployments, test the Master System of Record requirement first.** If Confidential Data has to leave the UAE, you need Central Bank approval and prior written customer consent — confirm both are achievable before the technical evaluation.
- **Check the provider's jurisdiction for supervisory-access barriers**, not just its certifications; a jurisdiction whose secrecy laws impede regulator access rules the arrangement out.
- **Ask for the transfer risk assessment inputs** the Saudi PDPL regime expects: purpose, nature and geography of processing, safeguards, recipient adequacy, and data minimisation.
- **Ask for evidence of human oversight and explainability**, not a [SOC 2 report](/glossary/soc-2-document-controls) or a [data-residency](/glossary/data-residency-in-document-ai) attestation — that's what the CBUAE AI principles actually describe.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does SAMA prohibit Saudi banks from using cloud-based document AI?

No, but it inverts the default. Cloud services are expected to run in-Kingdom, and using cloud services located outside Saudi Arabia requires explicit SAMA approval. Where the arrangement is material — as core KYC or underwriting extraction usually is — a prior no-objection for the outsourcing itself applies as a separate requirement.

### Can a UAE bank send customer documents to an offshore document AI API?

Only with both Central Bank approval and the customer's prior written consent, and never to a jurisdiction whose secrecy or other laws would restrict supervisory access to the data. The bank's Master System of Record, including all Confidential Data, must also remain continuously maintained within the UAE.

### Is a data-residency commitment enough for SAMA and CBUAE compliance for document AI?

No. Residency describes where documents are stored; both regimes also govern where processing happens, who approved it, and whether a supervisor can reach the data and the provider. A vendor that can name a storage region but not an inference location has answered only part of the question.

## The Takeaway

The Gulf's two largest financial regulators start from onshore as the default and treat offshore processing as an exception the institution must justify, document, and — in the UAE's case — obtain a customer's written consent for. A document AI vendor that runs inference inside the institution's own environment never enters that exception process, which is a materially shorter path than assembling approvals for a cross-border pipeline that a supervisor may still decline to accept.
