Every cloud document AI API asks a bank or insurer to make the same trade, usually without saying so directly: hand a KYC packet, a claims file, or a loan application to an endpoint the institution doesn't operate, in exchange for extraction that would otherwise take a human reviewer minutes to do by hand. The productivity trade is real. So is the compliance risk it creates — not because the API is insecure, but because "send the document to a third party for processing" is exactly the kind of ICT outsourcing arrangement DORA, GDPR, and a growing list of regional frameworks were written to scrutinize, and most procurement reviews still evaluate a document AI vendor as a software purchase rather than as what it actually is: a subcontracted processor of regulated customer data.

## What Makes a Cloud Document AI API a Compliance Risk for Banks and Insurers?

A cloud document AI API is a compliance risk for the same reason any outsourced processing of regulated data is a compliance risk: the institution remains accountable for what happens to the document, but it no longer controls where the processing happens or who else touches it along the way. Storage location is usually well documented — a regional bucket, a named data center, a line item in the vendor's data processing addendum. Inference location is the part that goes missing. The API call that actually reads a bank statement or a claims form is frequently routed to a general-purpose frontier model hosted by a different company than the one being paid, running in a jurisdiction nobody explicitly agreed to in writing. That gap between the vendor relationship on paper and the actual processing chain in production is where the risk sits — it's the same underlying problem [the sovereign AI gap](/blog/sovereign-ai-gap) describes at the architecture level, showing up here as a concrete procurement and contracting question rather than an abstract one.

## Where Does the Compliance Risk Actually Come From?

### Sub-Processor Chains Are Longer Than the Contract Suggests

Most document AI platforms marketed to regulated industries are built on top of someone else's model. The platform vendor handles the UI, the workflow, the review queue — but the extraction call itself is frequently a pass-through to a third-party model provider's inference API. That makes the platform vendor a reseller of another company's processing, and the sub-processor terms governing where that inference actually runs belong to the model provider, not the platform the bank signed a contract with. A single vendor relationship can quietly become a three- or four-party processing chain, and [data residency in document AI](/glossary/data-residency-in-document-ai) programs built around "where is the document stored" have no mechanism for tracking a chain that long.

### Accuracy Reviews Don't Catch This — Procurement Does, or Doesn't

The reason this risk survives evaluation is that most vendor bake-offs test accuracy and latency, not the sub-processor chain. A document AI API can score well on an accuracy benchmark and still route every page through infrastructure that would fail a regulator's outsourcing review if anyone had actually asked the question. Accuracy and compliance are evaluated by different teams on different timelines, and a vendor rarely volunteers the answer to a question procurement didn't ask.

## What Does DORA Require When a Bank Outsources Document AI to the Cloud?

For any EU or UK financial institution, this stops being a theoretical concern under the Digital Operational Resilience Act. [Regulation (EU) 2022/2554](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2554/oj/eng) — DORA — sets out in Article 30 the minimum contractual content required for any arrangement with an ICT third-party provider supporting a critical or important function, and two of those requirements land directly on a cloud document AI API: the contract must state whether subcontracting of the service is permitted and, if so, under what conditions, and it must specify the locations — regions or countries — where the service is to be provided and where the data is to be processed. A document AI vendor that can't name, in writing, every jurisdiction a document's contents pass through during inference — including the model provider's own infrastructure — hasn't satisfied Article 30's basic contractual bar, regardless of how accurate the extraction is.

## Does GDPR Apply to a Cloud Vendor's Inference Call?

Yes, and it applies at the moment of inference, not just at the moment of storage. Under [Article 44 of the GDPR](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-44-gdpr/), any transfer of personal data to a third country is only lawful if the conditions in the regulation's transfer chapter are met — and that obligation extends explicitly to onward transfers, meaning a sub-processor's sub-processor is still in scope, not just the vendor the institution contracted with directly. Most KYC packets, claims files, and loan applications are full of personal data — names, addresses, national ID numbers, financial history — so a single API call that routes a document through an out-of-region inference endpoint can trigger Article 44 obligations the institution never evaluated, because the transfer wasn't visible in the vendor relationship it thought it was managing. [GDPR data extraction compliance](/glossary/gdpr-data-extraction-compliance) as a discipline exists precisely because "processing" under the regulation captures every model inference call over personal data, not just the database it's eventually written to.

## What Should Banks and Insurers Ask Before Signing a Cloud Document AI Vendor?

- **Ask the vendor to name every jurisdiction the document's contents pass through**, including any third-party model provider — a regional storage guarantee doesn't answer this on its own.
- **Ask whether subcontracting is disclosed and conditioned in the contract itself**, per DORA Article 30, not just described informally in a sales conversation.
- **Ask what "processing" covers under the vendor's GDPR terms** — does it include the inference call itself, or only the data stored before and after it?
- **Ask for a [document audit trail](/glossary/document-audit-trail)**, not just a [SOC 2 report](/glossary/soc-2-document-controls) — a SOC 2 attestation describes the control environment; a per-document trail proves where a specific file's processing actually happened.
- **Ask what deployment options exist beyond the default cloud API** — cloud-hosted, within the institution's own infrastructure, or fully on-premise — and whether "zero-egress" means the document never leaves that boundary at all, or just that it's encrypted in transit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a cloud document AI API automatically non-compliant with DORA or GDPR?

No — not automatically, but it carries real compliance risk if the vendor can't answer specific questions in writing: every jurisdiction the document passes through, whether subcontracting is disclosed, and what "processing" covers under its data terms. Many cloud vendors can answer these; the risk is in institutions not asking before signing.

### Does storing data in the EU satisfy GDPR if the AI model runs elsewhere?

Not by itself. GDPR Article 44 governs transfers of personal data to a third country, and that obligation follows the data through onward transfers to sub-processors — so an inference call routed outside the storage region can trigger transfer obligations even when the document itself is stored in-region.

### What's the difference between a data processing addendum and a DORA-compliant contract?

A standard data processing addendum typically covers how personal data is handled and secured. DORA Article 30 goes further for critical or important ICT functions: it requires the contract to specify whether subcontracting is permitted, under what conditions, and the actual locations — regions or countries — where the service is provided and data is processed, not just how it's protected.
